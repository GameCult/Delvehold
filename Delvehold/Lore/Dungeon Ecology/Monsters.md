---
title: Monsters
description: “Monster” is an economic permission, not a biological category.
---

Calling a dangerous organism a **monster** makes killing it socially acceptable and its remains available for use. The label groups creatures by how civilization treats them, rather than by biological kinship.

That distinction matters when industry puts a price on their bodies. Classification helps determine what people are permitted to do: harvest a creature without its consent, pay compensation for damaging one treated as property, or exterminate one classified as a production hazard.

## Where monsters come from

Dungeon cores produce or cultivate many monsters. Others migrate toward mana-rich environments. In those places, accelerated variation produces regional forms faster than ordinary evolution would permit.

Creatures commonly called monsters include slimes, giant insects, kobolds, trolls, basilisks, mimics, owlbears, elementals, giant spiders, and several kinds of undead.

## What they do in a dungeon

Dungeon creatures have roles beyond fighting adventurers. They may:

- graze or break down dead material;
- defend the dungeon or act as mobile sensory organs;
- pollinate plants, carry seeds, or haul materials;
- live as parasites or symbionts;
- keep other populations in check;
- serve as experiments in responding to recurring intruders.

Serving a dungeon does not necessarily mean being mentally controlled by it. A dungeon can influence creatures through the conditions of their habitat, chemical signals, or instinctive attraction. Other relationships involve negotiated cooperation. In some cases, the dungeon fabricates the creature directly.

## Why adventurers meet hostility

Adventurers enter habitat that its inhabitants defend. The relentless hostility they experience is real, but it tells them only part of what those creatures are doing.

A basilisk may be protecting a crystal nursery. That does not make it morally benevolent. Nor does killing it establish that the nursery belongs to the killer.

## How creatures adapt to industry

Industrial pressure changes dungeon ecologies. They produce mana-eating moulds, rune-disrupting insects, crystal-boring worms, and creatures resistant to standardized spells. The next generation attacks machines that survived the previous one.

Those changes do not, by themselves, establish how they arose. A core may have designed an adaptation, or it may only have retained a change that proved effective.
