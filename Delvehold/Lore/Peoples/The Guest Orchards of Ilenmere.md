title: The Guest Orchards of Ilenmere
description: An elven lakeshore culture that welcomes a little of another season into the present.
---

*Canon candidate.*

At Ilenmere, an elven child can eat a fresh plum in the snow because someone has spent the afternoon tending a spell around one branch. The family gathers beneath it with blankets and bowls. Whoever holds the working eats first; otherwise an attentive host can finish an evening hungry, having provided everyone else with summer.

Ilenmere is a proposed lake district of sheltered orchard terraces and reed-fringed fishing villages. Its people live on fish, beans, nuts, and ordinary harvests, trading dried fruit and young trees along the shore. They belong to the varied cultures of the [[Elves]], and their pride is a magical gardening practice called **guesting**: inviting one part of a tree into a season the surrounding countryside has yet to reach.

## A season small enough to tend

Guesting follows the ordinary work of [[Mana and Pattern|spellcasting]]. A gardener gathers mana, traces the branching form with cord and hand gestures, steadies the intended growth, and releases the spell into the chosen bough. Continued attention keeps that small patch warm and guides budding, flowering, or ripening. Fruit already picked remains fruit; when attention ends, the sheltered air cools and the living branch resumes growth in the weather around it. A gardener must wrap tender growth before leaving it to frost.

The spell draws on the tree's water and stored nourishment. It cannot supply a winter's food from a bare stick. Gardeners reserve different boughs for successive visits and give heavily worked trees ordinary growing seasons to recover. Blossom is easier than fruit. Children begin with a single bud and learn to stop while it is healthy, a skill considerably less popular than making it open.

Expertise lies in knowing a particular tree: where its roots stay wet, which graft tires early, how much fruit it can carry. Apprentices work beside fishers and growers before anyone trusts them with winter plums. People admire a gardener who leaves a tree vigorous enough for someone else to learn on.

Guesting accompanies births, reunions, and evenings with no special excuse. Couples sometimes choose a flowering branch together, then return to its tree through the years. At funerals, relatives tend the blossom the dead person liked. Its scent helps them remember; the working offers no speech from the dead. Visitors are often surprised by the laughter under funeral trees, until someone explains whose dreadful grafting they are discussing.

## The tree has other company

Many Ilenmere households understand tree, orchard, and lakeshore as persons belonging within larger persons. Before a first guesting, they speak aloud what they hope to grow and leave time to watch the tree through several changes of weather. Their interpretations differ. A gardener may describe a vigorous new shoot as welcome; her brother may insist that knowing when a tree can spare fruit is sufficient courtesy without claiming to hear it answer.

Their [[Gods and Religion|religious life]] also includes offerings to gods of weather and death. At the common harvest meal, families bring fruit ripened in the open season. They thank the powers whose gifts arrive across the whole shore, including gardens without a mage. Some devout guesters save their finest winter work for private pleasure and consider this shared autumn meal the year's greater occasion. Guesting itself is a learned spell, available to people who pray differently or seldom pray at all.

## Taking summer away

The most contentious ambition in Ilenmere is to teach guesting abroad. Gardener Seli grows small plum trees in tubs and teaches sailors the simplest blossom working. She wants emigrants, including her own daughter, to have flowering tables wherever they settle. Tuition and tree sales could also support apprentices who cannot inherit an orchard.

Her former teacher, Arven, offers to train anyone who stays through a full growing season. He objects to Seli's shortened lessons: tubs conceal root trouble, and an impressive first flowering can sell a sick tree. Yet his apprenticeship requires time away from paid work. Several families would gladly accept less spectacular blossoms if learning them did not require their children to remain at home.

Seli's daughter wants to raise unfamiliar fruit on another shore. She has asked for seeds instead of the plum tree her mother has already prepared.

## Unresolved hooks

- Seli's first travelling class returns with healthy trees that refuse to flower under her usual working. Will she go abroad to learn what changed, leaving her new pupils behind?
- Arven offers a fisher's apprentice free instruction during the busiest fishing season. The apprentice asks him to teach aboard the boat.
- A bereaved household wants to replace its failing funeral tree with a vigorous young graft. One sibling wants to let that particular place of remembrance end.
