---
title: The Long Orchard
description: A halfling nursery community saving to plant a second village.
---

> Canon candidate: Ledger pass, 20260905-w001-3.

At Brindlebank, a halfling village on low hills above a fertile river plain, even someone who owns no field can spend twenty years making a better pear. The village keeps an orchard for experiments, maintained from the sale of young trees. Residents call it the Long Orchard. Belonging means there will be someone to water your seedlings while you earn your living elsewhere, and somewhere to bring the first fruit when it finally comes.

This is one community among the farming and trading settlements of the [[Halflings]]. Its nurseries supply smallholders and commercial growers, including the market gardens outside [[Humans|human towns]]. Buyers want trees that survive transplanting, bear useful fruit, and suit their soil. Brindlebank sells young stock with roots wrapped in damp straw; experienced planters travel with the larger orders. Dried pears, cider, and fresh fruit pay the nearer bills. Vegetable plots and purchased grain feed the village while its more ambitious work grows.

The experimental ground belongs to the village. Families also own orchards, rent plots, or live by nursery wages. Every resident household can tend a few trial trees on the common ground, including households formed by newcomers. Space is limited, so older trials eventually have to justify keeping their row. A good cooking pear may deserve room beside a beautiful eating pear. A tree that has produced nothing edible for thirty years usually has one very eloquent defender.

Children learn to raise seedlings and to graft: joining a twig from a favoured tree to a rooted young tree so that its fruit can be grown again. Seedlings offer uncertain possibilities; grafting multiplies a success. The first lesson is often how to tie a label that will survive winter. Brindlebank has lost more pedigrees to poor string than anyone likes admitting.

People admire patient observation, but delight matters too. At autumn tastings, slices circulate before anyone announces whose tree bore them. Growers want to hear an unprompted request for another piece. A successful variety takes its name from its grower, a household joke, or somebody the grower misses. Widowed neighbours keep tending unfinished trials together. Young people leave for travelling work and send home twigs, descriptions, and extravagant assessments of foreign lunches. Plenty come back to pressing, carting, or raising children without ever wanting to breed a tree.

The village's present ambition is a second orchard settlement on cooler upland ground, growing fruit that ripens after Brindlebank's harvest. Two harvest seasons would spread the rush of picking and give buyers a longer supply. Nursery earnings are accumulating toward the purchase of a grazed hillside offered for sale. A second village would also give adult children room to establish households without waiting for a parent to die.

Agreement frays over what to plant. Established growers want proven varieties earning money quickly. Nursery labourers want a substantial experimental orchard from the beginning: they have spent years propagating other people's successes and want ground for their own. They can afford neither private land nor several fruitless seasons. The village could support them, but doing so would postpone the new settlement's income. The tenant shepherd currently grazing the hillside wants enough pasture retained to move with it; the seller's price assumes every usable slope becomes orchard.

Enchanted refrigeration, already familiar in halfling farming, has made Brindlebank's fresh fruit easier to sell at a distance. It has also made dependable, handsome varieties more profitable than awkward local favourites. Meanwhile, reports of crop mutation among the [[Surface Symptoms|surface disturbances]] give the breeders another worry. One extraordinary harvest cannot show whether a new trait will persist. They want several seasons of fruit from propagated trees before asking a family to plant a whole field with them. Buyers anxious about failing crops want answers sooner.

## Hooks left open

- The upland seller will wait until spring. Brindlebank has enough money to buy the land, but only if it spends the fund intended to feed the first settlers before their trees bear.
- A nursery worker's late pear may suit the new hillside. She wants to move there; her ageing collaborator wants their trial to stay within walking distance of home.
- The tenant shepherd offers winter manure and summer grazing between widely spaced trees. Accepting would change both the planting plan and who can make a living in the new village.
