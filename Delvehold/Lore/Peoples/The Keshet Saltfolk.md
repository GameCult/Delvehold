# The Keshet Saltfolk

*Canon candidate.*

The Keshet saltfolk live beside a shallow inland basin, where summer sun leaves white crusts across the mud. Their settlements stand on the gravel slopes above it, close to freshwater springs. Most are [[Humans|human]], descended from salt workers and mule carriers who began staying through the winter. A Keshet child learns early that a basin can be covered in something valuable and still have nothing for supper.

Three conditions sustain these towns: salty seepage that can be led into shallow drying pans, drinkable water on the higher slopes, and a mule route to grain-growing valleys. The springs supply households and small patches of beans; they cannot support broad grain fields. Salt pays for flour, fodder, timber and iron. Dung and thorn scrub fuel the cooking fires. Houses have thick earth walls on stone footings, and roof beams are reused through several generations of rebuilding.

The most welcome sound in Keshet is grain pouring into a bin. Returning carriers bring barley and wheat from neighbouring [[Halflings|halfling]] farming settlements, along with letters, seed cakes and whatever somebody's brother was absolutely certain could be bought more cheaply down-valley. Families keep long tables beside their outdoor ovens. On a carrier's first evening home, bread is torn while still too hot, and children search the baggage for gifts before helping to unload it. The flour must come first; this instruction has survived generations without much improvement in compliance.

Work at the pans begins before the glare becomes painful. Families repair low clay banks, guide brine into the shallows, scrape the dried salt and heap it beneath covers against rain. Children learn to distinguish clean crystals from grit, but also to inspect grain for damp. Being easy to cheat over supper is considered a serious gap in an otherwise excellent education.

An established household usually works several inherited pans. Newcomers can make pans along the basin's unused edge, with neighbouring households helping on the understanding that help will be returned. This costs weeks of labour before there is anything to sell. People without that time work for other families through the drying season. Some prefer the dependable payment and freedom from repairs; others spend years saving for banks of mud which their employers' children receive as wedding presents.

The largest differences in wealth come from transport. A mule needs feed even when it carries nothing, and the climb out of the basin limits every load. Families with animals buy neighbours' salt and sell it down-valley. Experienced carriers know whose granary stays dry, who will extend credit after a bad season, and which household has room for a sick traveller. Pan workers often marry into those distant households. A person can belong deeply to Keshet while spending half the year elsewhere.

People admire a carrier who remembers what everyone wanted. They also admire a home where nothing essential has to be borrowed: a sound roof, a full grain bin, enough cooking pots when cousins arrive. These ambitions do not always fit the same purse.

Adra, who hires out at the pans, wants the towns to widen the steepest stretch of the mule path for small carts. Her husband repairs carts down-valley. They have saved enough for a donkey, which could pull more salt in a cart than it could carry on its back. With a usable road, they hope to build a pan and live together. Several pan owners will contribute grain to feed the road crew. They expect cheaper freight and larger sales.

Bek, a carrier with six mules, argues for spending the grain on fodder stores instead. Carts would favour the direct route to the largest market, passing above two villages where his family has traded for decades. Those visits supply credit and winter lodging as well as customers. He offers Adra a place on his next journey. She has travelled with him before and likes the people he wants her to meet again. What she wants now is her husband at her own oven.

## Hooks left

- Adra's husband brings a narrow cart up as far as it will go. The road supporters must unload it and decide whether to begin cutting the bend before the salt harvest.
- One of Bek's valley hosts offers timber for the road if its builders include a branch to her village. Her sons have already marked the proposed turning.
- An unusually wet drying season recalls reports of [[Surface Symptoms|disrupted weather]] elsewhere. Nobody has established a cause; the immediate question is which household can spare grain until the pans dry.
