---
title: The Sweet Thanks
description: A proposed local devotion to Mereth, celestial goddess of love.
---

*Canon candidate; proposed locality, deity, and observance.*

At Mereth's shrine in Tallowbridge, worshippers bring something sweet that a loved person taught them to enjoy. The offering might be a honey cake, a spoonful of plum preserve, or the expensive sugared seeds a grandmother kept in her sewing basket. After the prayer, everyone eats. People remember who first gave them a taste, and sometimes discover that the treasured family recipe came from the baker two doors away.

Tallowbridge is a small surface market town in a fertile farming district. [[Humans]] and [[Halflings]] have lived beside its grain mills for generations, selling flour, keeping shops, and farming the surrounding land. The Sweet Thanks began among households who brought harvest gifts to the shrine; confectioners now sell little portions for people who cannot spare a whole jar. The shrine buys flour and lamp oil from ordinary donations. Its monthly gathering has become somewhere people go to hear about one another's lives, including lives that have moved beyond the town.

Mereth belongs to the [[Gods and Religion|celestial gods]], with love as her domain. Her local image is a woman tasting something from an open hand. Children learn her prayer while passing a dish: “Thank you for the gladness I could not have made alone.” They also learn to leave enough for the next person. The second lesson takes longer.

Worshippers ask Mereth for courage to show affection, for tenderness after an argument, and for the chance to love again. Some bring the same food every month. Others arrive with a newly acquired taste and enjoy being questioned. Friendship and love between relatives have their own crowded place at the table; a marriage announcement need not displace a child's proud account of acquiring a friend.

The distinctive teaching here concerns affection that has ended. Priest Adrel teaches that Mereth may receive thanks for the happiness of a relationship whose continuation would now make both people miserable. A former lover's favorite sweet can therefore sit among wedding cakes and gifts remembering the dead. For Adrel, this is a way to approach the goddess without having to call a whole portion of one's life a mistake. People who have separated sometimes return to worship because they can finally say an honest prayer there.

Adrel's older sister Tirva finds this teaching too easy. She brings pear paste her husband learned to make during a difficult year of their marriage. She wants the young to hear about the work that followed their disappointment. She fears that thanking Mereth for a finished happiness lets people congratulate themselves before trying to repair anything. Adrel remembers how long she spent making that repair alone. Neither enjoys the other's version of the story.

There is no agreed answer to how much of a thanksgiving should be said aloud. Belis, a miller, has brought almond sweets since his separation from Hadrin. Hadrin introduced him to them and now sits across the room with a new partner. Belis wants to thank the goddess for five good years. Hadrin has asked him to stop naming those years in public: the congregation keeps watching for a reconciliation. A silent offering would let Belis keep the devotion, but he resents having to whisper about something he remembers without shame.

The shrine promises no sign that will settle their quarrel. Its food and prayers are worship; preparing them teaches none of the operations of [[Mana and Pattern|spellcasting]]. Adrel's teaching is an interpretation of what Mereth cherishes. The goddess's existence gives that question weight without making the priest's answer certain.

- Hadrin has offered to bring the almond sweets this month if Belis will let the offering pass without a speech. Belis has already written one.
- Tirva wants her wedding thanksgiving to include the year she lived apart from her husband. He has invited their children expecting a celebration of unbroken devotion.
- A young confectioner has made a bitter orange sweet for a friendship she hopes to mend. Her former friend has agreed to attend, but asked for separate seats.
