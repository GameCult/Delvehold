---
title: The Guest Kitchens of Claymead
description: A river settlement where a welcome includes somewhere to cook for yourself.
---

In Claymead, people showing a prospective home to a lover often begin with the guest kitchen. It may be only a little tiled room opening onto the yard, with a stove, a water crock and somewhere to sit. A good one catches morning light. You can imagine your brother staying there with his children, or a friend arriving after a bad marriage, without either becoming an extra person to feed at precisely the right hour.

Claymead stands on a low river terrace among grain fields and clay pits. Its households include [[Halflings]], [[Humans]] and [[Orcs]], with families established through farm work, tile making and river carriage. Autumn once brought more hired harvesters and boat crews than the settlement could seat at its evening tables. Farmers added cooking shelters beside their lodgings. Visiting workers could eat before dawn, prepare familiar food and share a pot without waking the household. Some stayed, married locally or bought neighbouring plots. Their cooking shelters became rooms worth keeping after the harvest.

The guest kitchen spread from paid lodging into family hospitality over several generations. Claymead still sells tiles and grain, and relatives still arrive for stretches of seasonal work, but people also come simply to spend a fortnight near someone they miss. The first evening usually belongs to the host. Next morning the visitor finds flour, salt, fuel and a filled crock. Later provisions are discussed according to the length and reason for the stay. Charging a working lodger is ordinary; silently running up a bill for an invited aunt would get a household talked about for years.

The pleasure is in the second invitation. A visitor can knock on the main house and ask its inhabitants to breakfast in their own yard. Children are taught to knock and wait at the guest door, even though they ran freely through that room before the visitors arrived. Grandparents sometimes invite just one grandchild to lunch, giving a crowded household a small, fiercely anticipated appointment. Hosts learn which guests want company while chopping and which will emerge when the food is ready.

These manners are learned locally. An orcish boatwright who grew up here may find a week of being served every meal unbearable; her orcish husband from upriver considers being left to find the kettle an oddly strenuous welcome. The distinction also runs through families. Some residents save for a generous single kitchen where nobody eats alone. They invite visitors to pull up a chair and resent the suggestion that their hospitality is unfinished brickwork.

Two kitchens take fuel, cleaning and space. The smallest houses borrow a neighbour's guest room when several relatives arrive. Established families admire the resulting easy movement between houses; tenants sometimes hear that admiration from people who have never had to carry their breakfast through rain. Buyers with money increasingly ask for guest kitchens with separate street doors. An adult child could live there for years, earn separately and still come across for supper. Parents disagree about whether that is the home they hoped to offer.

At Hadrin's house, that question has arrived with his older sister's pans. Hadrin, a human tiler, has offered Maret the guest rooms permanently after her hands became too painful for regular paid sewing. He proposes removing the small stove and widening the doorway so he can carry meals straight through. Maret wants a lighter kettle and a lower shelf. She still likes making breakfast, including breakfast for him. On difficult mornings she wants him to bring food and leave the stove alone. Hadrin remembers the last kettle she dropped. The mason is coming at the end of the week, and Maret has put her favourite pan back on its hook.

## Open hooks

- Maret asks Hadrin's halfling neighbour to help her try a lighter kettle before the mason arrives. Hadrin has already paid for the wider doorway.
- A young couple can afford either a guest kitchen or a second bedroom. One has promised a widowed parent a place to stay; the other wants children.
- A returning seasonal lodger invites the host family to breakfast, then asks to rent the rooms through winter. Their youngest child has already set a place for her at supper.
