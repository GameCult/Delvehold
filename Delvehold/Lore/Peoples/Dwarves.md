Most dwarves live in underground settlements connected by routes through a mountain range. Together, these settlements form [[The Greathold]]. Dwarven life depends on work that later generations can trust: a gallery that stays dry, an alloy made correctly, an oath that still serves the grandchildren of those who swore it. Such work earns lasting praise.

Repetition, precision, and repair already mattered in this culture before iterative runes arrived. The new runic industry grew among people accustomed to testing a process, repeating it, and keeping its results in working order.

## The Greathold

Each dwarven Hold occupies a **Vault**: an immense chamber or system of chambers made by a dungeon, with its own climate, ecology, and core. People have turned ancient galleries into streets and built cisterns in chambers whose original purpose nobody knows. Routes connect inhabited Vaults along the mountain spine. Settlement has not put the dungeon safely elsewhere; the Deep World continues beneath the paving and sometimes through it.

Long before industrialization, the Greathold's deep gates were the world's principal point of entry for adventurers. Holds already knew how to clear chambers, secure routes, record dungeon behaviour, and contract foreign delvers. These were ordinary responsibilities of settled life. Industry later gained an advantage from the knowledge and access they provided.

Industrial power now drains dangerous mines, heats homes, preserves food through winter, and carries millions along lit rails. When a Hold's lift office says its engines cannot simply stop, residents have warm homes and working transport that help explain why.

## Becoming a citizen

Dwarven civic adulthood requires [[Mastership and Citizenship|Mastership]]. Reaching a certain age or having dwarven ancestors does not make someone a full citizen.

An apprentice must produce an accepted **Masterwork**, establish an independent workshop, and take responsibility for its civic seal. The seal gives the workshop its place in civic decisions, with its Master answerable for it.

This passage is open to people of other ancestries. An elf or goblin who completes it carries the same civic seal as a dwarf. Dwarven birth alone grants none.

## Disputes within industry

Evidence about possible cognition in dungeon cores is dividing the institutions that depend on extraction. Rune College authorities delay publishing cognition measurements; the measurements then reach the public through union newspapers. What they mean, and what people should do about them, remain matters of dispute.

Mine organizers use the findings to challenge conditions on core farms. That does not mean they agree extraction should end. Traditional workshops reprint the same evidence against industrial consortiums while still depending on those consortiums' district furnaces through winter. People can press a case against industrial practices and still need the machinery running.

## Religious inheritance

Older religious practice gives miners specific instructions: listen before cutting, seal a gallery when it is exhausted, and leave payment in abandoned workings.

Industrial theologians look to the primordial smith's hammer for permission to reshape stone. Older carvings also show the smith leaving an offering after finishing his work. The inherited image includes both the act of making and an obligation when it is done.

## Dependence and fear

Calls from abroad to stop extraction reach districts whose heat, water, and food depend on deep power. For their inhabitants, abrupt closure raises immediate questions about how those necessities will continue.

Consortiums use that dependence to justify further expansion. Each winter makes the case for an abrupt shutdown harder to argue, because the services at risk are the ones people need to get through it.
